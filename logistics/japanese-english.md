# Japanese-Accented English (Katakana English)

Japanese has borrowed thousands of English words, but they're filtered through the Japanese sound system. The result is "katakana English" — English words pronounced with Japanese phonetics. Once you get the pattern, you can:

1. **Understand what a Japanese speaker is saying** when they use an English word
2. **Be understood more easily** by saying English words the Japanese way

---

## The Core Rules

**1. Almost every syllable ends in a vowel**
Japanese syllables are almost always consonant + vowel (ka, ki, ku, ke, ko, etc.). So consonants at the end of English words get a vowel added.
- "milk" → **mi-ru-ku** (3 syllables)
- "desk" → **de-su-ku**
- "park" → **pa-a-ku**
- "ticket" → **chi-ke-tto**

**2. L and R are the same sound**
There's one sound in Japanese that covers both. It's made by briefly touching the tongue to the roof of the mouth — somewhere between an English L and R. This is why "rice" and "lice" sound identical.
- "restaurant" → **re-su-to-ran**
- "hotel" → **ho-te-ru**
- Say R without rolling it too hard, or L with a slightly lifted tongue — either gets you close enough.

**3. V becomes B**
Japanese has no V sound.
- "vodka" → **bo-do-ka**
- "convenience" → **kon-bi-ni**
- "service" → **sa-a-bi-su**

**4. Long vowels are drawn out**
A long dash over a vowel (ā, ī, ū) means hold it longer. On the street this sounds like the vowel is said twice.
- "coffee" → **ko-o-hii** (not "koffee")
- "beer" → **bii-ru**
- "cake" → **ke-e-ki**
- "taxi" → **ta-ku-shii**

**5. "Si" → "shi", "ti" → "chi", "tu" → "tsu"**
These are just how Japanese phonetics work.
- "service" → **sa-a-bi-su**
- "ticket" → **chi-ke-tto** (ti → chi)
- "pasta" → **pa-su-ta**

**6. Words get shortened**
Long English words are often truncated in daily speech.
- "convenience store" → **konbini** (コンビニ)
- "smartphone" → **sumaho** (スマホ)
- "personal computer" → **pasokon** (パソコン)
- "air conditioner" → **eakon** (エアコン)
- "remote control" → **rimokon** (リモコン)

## Practical Tips

**When you're not being understood:**
Say the English word more slowly, with equal stress on each syllable, and add a vowel sound after any final consonant. "Ticket" → "chi-ke-tto." "Credit card" → "ku-re-ji-tto ka-a-do."

**When you can't understand them:**
If someone says something that sounds vaguely like English but mangled, try mentally removing vowels from the end of each syllable and listen for the English word underneath.

**False friends to watch out for:**
- **"Mansion"** (マンション) = apartment, not a mansion
- **"Viking"** (バイキング) = buffet/all-you-can-eat, not Vikings
- **"Cunning"** (カンニング) = cheating on a test, not sly/clever
- **"Handle"** (ハンドル) = steering wheel, not a door handle
- **"Naive"** (ナイーブ) = sensitive/fragile, not gullible
- **"Hot"** (ホット) = specifically warm drinks ("hot coffee"), not general hotness
- **"Cooler"** (クーラー) = air conditioner, not a beer cooler
- **"Free"** (フリー) = unrestricted/available, not always "no cost" — use *muryō* (無料) for free of charge

## How to Use This in the Field

At a sushi counter with no English: point at the fish in the table above, or say the romaji from the ordering guide. If you want to ask for something on the blackboard, pointing works fine — say **"kore wo kudasai"** (this one, please) while pointing.

At a hotel or shop: lean into the English word but say it with Japanese phonetics. "Checkout?" said normally may get a blank look. "Che-ku-a-u-to?" lands immediately.

On the train: station names are almost always announced in both Japanese and English-accented English. Listen for the English — it'll be there, just slower and more evenly stressed.
