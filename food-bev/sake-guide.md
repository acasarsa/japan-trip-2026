## What Sake Actually Is

Sake (日本酒, *nihonshu*) is brewed from rice, water, yeast, and koji mold — closer to beer in process than wine, despite being called "rice wine." Alcohol content is typically 14–16%, higher than wine. It can be served cold, room temperature, or warm depending on the style.

The word "sake" (酒) in Japanese just means alcohol generally. If you want sake specifically, say **nihonshu** (日本酒) — "Japanese alcohol."

## The Main Types

### By Polish Level (the most important distinction)

The more the rice is polished (milled down), the more delicate and fruity the sake tends to be. Less polishing = more rice flavor, earthiness, complexity.

| Type | Polish | Flavor |
|------|--------|--------|
| **Junmai Daiginjo** 純米大吟醸 | 50%+ milled away | Delicate, floral, fruity — the "fine wine" of sake |
| **Junmai Ginjo** 純米吟醸 | 40%+ milled away | Fruity and clean, some complexity |
| **Junmai** 純米 | No minimum | Full rice flavor, savory, umami-rich |
| **Honjozo** 本醸造 | 30%+ milled away | Light, slightly dry, easy to drink warm |

**Junmai** means "pure rice" — no added distilled alcohol. Non-junmai versions have a small addition of alcohol to adjust flavor and aroma, which isn't a negative thing.

### By Style (more useful at a bar)

**Nigori** 濁り — Cloudy, unfiltered. Creamy, sweet, low alcohol. Good entry point. Shake the bottle before pouring.

**Kimoto** 生酛 — Traditional brewing method using wild lactic acid bacteria. Richer, earthier, more complex. Often described as funky, yogurt-y, or mushroom-y. These age well and reward slow drinking.

**Yamahai** 山廃 — Similar to kimoto but even more hands-off. Wilder fermentation. Bold, savory, sometimes game-y. The most "interesting" style if you want something unusual.

**Muroka** 無濾過 — Unfiltered (but not cloudy like nigori). Preserves more raw flavor from the brewery.

**Nama** 生 — Unpasteurized, fresh sake. Must be refrigerated. Bright, lively, slightly effervescent feel. Seasonal — harder to find outside Japan.

**Koshu** 古酒 — Aged sake. Amber-colored, nutty, caramel-y, almost like sherry. Unusual and polarizing.

**Sparkling sake** 発泡性日本酒 — Growing in popularity. Dry and effervescent. Some are made like champagne, others are just carbonated.

## Dry vs Sweet

- **Karakuchi** 辛口 — Dry
- **Amakuchi** 甘口 — Sweet
- **Nihonshu-do** 日本酒度 — The sake meter value. Positive number = drier, negative = sweeter. +5 and above is quite dry. -5 and below is noticeably sweet.
- **Sando** 酸度 — Acidity. Higher acidity = more crisp and dry-feeling even if the sugar level is moderate.

If you want something dry and complex, ask for **karakuchi de, kimoto ka yamahai ga arimasu ka?** — "Do you have anything dry, kimoto or yamahai style?"

## Temperature

- **Reishu / Hiya** 冷酒/冷や — Cold (most ginjo/daiginjo are best this way)
- **Jouon** 常温 — Room temperature
- **Nurukan** ぬる燗 — Warm (~40°C) — good for junmai and honjozo
- **Atsukan** 熱燗 — Hot (~50°C) — bold, robust styles only

Delicate, floral sakes (ginjo, daiginjo) should be cold. Earthier, richer sakes (junmai, kimoto, yamahai) are often better warm. A good sake bar will recommend a temperature.

## What to Ask For

**"I want something dry and interesting"**
辛口で、個性的なものはありますか？
*Karakuchi de, kosei-teki na mono wa arimasu ka?*

**"Do you have kimoto or yamahai?"**
生酛か山廃はありますか？
*Kimoto ka yamahai wa arimasu ka?*

**"Do you have a sake flight?"**
飲み比べセットはありますか？
*Nomi-kurabe setto wa arimasu ka?*

**"What do you recommend?"**
おすすめは何ですか？
*Osusume wa nan desu ka?*

**"Something unusual or funky"**
個性的で面白いものはありますか？
*Kosei-teki de omoshiroi mono wa arimasu ka?*

**"Cold, please"**
冷たいのをください
*Tsumetai no wo kudasai*

**"Warm, please"**
燗をつけてください
*Kan wo tsukete kudasai*

**"One more of the same"**
同じものをもう一つ
*Onaji mono wo mou hitotsu*

## Sake Labels — What to Look For

Labels worth seeking out (widely respected, available at good bars):

**Funky / Natural / Kimoto style:**
- **Tamagawa** 玉川 (Kyoto) — Kimoto specialist, wild and complex. Their "Time Machine" aged sake is extraordinary.
- **Tedorigawa** 手取川 (Ishikawa) — Yamahaiの老舗, rich and savory
- **Yuho** 遊穂 (Ishikawa) — Natural brewing, often described as the funkiest mainstream sake
- **Shichida** 七田 (Saga) — Junmai heavy, earthy and full-bodied
- **Amabuki** 天吹 (Saga) — Uses flower yeasts instead of standard brewing yeasts, very unusual aromatics

**Clean / Dry / Elegant:**
- **Dassai** 獺祭 (Yamaguchi) — The gateway sake, polished and approachable. Fine but everywhere.
- **Juyondai** 十四代 (Yamagata) — Nearly impossible to find, intensely sought after. If you see it, order it.
- **Kubota** 久保田 (Niigata) — Dry, clean, classic Niigata style
- **Hakkaisan** 八海山 (Niigata) — Crisp, cold-climate sake

**Sparkling / Modern:**
- **Mio** 澪 (Hyogo) — Sparkling sake, sweet and light, widely available
- **Awa Yuki** (various) — Dry sparkling sake, more serious than Mio
- **Ninki-ichi** 人気一 — Has a sparkling version

## On Cider in Japan

Japan has a small but growing craft cider scene — good news for you.

**The word issue:** サイダー (*saida*) in Japanese almost always means **fizzy soft drink** (like Sprite or 7-Up), NOT alcoholic cider. Asking for "cider" will get you a Sprite equivalent.

**What to ask for instead:**
- **シードル** (*shi-do-ru*) — Cidre, the French/European style, alcoholic. This is the word used at craft bars.
- **ハードサイダー** (*ha-do sai-da-a*) — Hard cider, less commonly used but understood.

**Where to find it:** Craft beer bars often carry Japanese ciders. Apple-growing regions like Aomori and Nagano produce most of Japan's craft cider. In Tokyo, look at craft bottle shops (Passione Vino, Antenna America, etc.) or bars that stock natural wine — they often carry Japanese cider alongside it.

Notable Japanese ciders:
- **Hirosaki Cidre** (Aomori) — Dry, serious cidre from Japan's apple capital
- **Kimura Cidre** (Aomori) — From a famous apple farmer, very limited
- **Nagano Cidre** — Various producers, increasingly available in Tokyo bars

Realistically: you'll find it at craft-focused bars but it's not on most standard menus. Sake will be far more accessible and interesting across all three cities.

## Sake vs Beer at a Sushi Counter

Traditional rule: sake is drunk during the *otsumami* (appetizer) phase before nigiri, not during. The logic is that both sake and sushi rice are made from rice — drinking sake during nigiri is said to dull the palate and create a "rice on rice" heaviness.

In practice: most people ignore this rule now. But if you want to do it properly — drink sake with the appetizers, switch to beer or tea during the nigiri course.
